2. Project Overview: Academic Evidence Investigator

The Academic Evidence Investigator was proposed as an intelligent research assistant for postgraduate students and early-career researchers. Its intended purpose is to help users organise research questions, identify relevant literature, examine evidence and prepare structured summaries.

Academic research requires more than generating fluent text. Researchers need to assess source quality, distinguish evidence from assumptions, recognise uncertainty and verify claims before relying on them in academic work.

Proposed agent architecture

1. Planner / Supervisor Agent

Intended to break a research goal into manageable tasks, coordinate the workflow and decide which activity should happen next.

2. Retrieval Agent

Intended to locate potentially relevant academic sources through services such as Semantic Scholar and Crossref. Retrieved records would need to be checked for accuracy and relevance.

3. Evidence Analysis / Critic Agent

Intended to examine evidence, identify gaps and inconsistencies, and recommend further investigation when information is insufficient.

4. Reporting Agent

Intended to organise findings into a structured report, distinguishing evidence, interpretation and unresolved questions.

Intended benefits and limitations

The proposed system could help researchers organise early-stage literature investigation. It should support rather than replace independent reading, source verification and academic judgement. The quality of its results would depend on the sources available, the methods used and the effectiveness of its evaluation.

3. Design and Implementation

Original design proposal

The group proposal considered a multi-agent architecture using LangGraph to coordinate tasks. It proposed Qwen2.5-7B through Hugging Face for language-model capabilities, Semantic Scholar and Crossref for literature discovery, JSON for structured information, SQLite for storage and Flask for a possible web interface.

These technologies describe the proposed design. They do not demonstrate that every component was integrated into the final implementation.

Practical implementation

The local starter project, named llm_planning_agent_starter, was run in a Python virtual environment. The recorded program accepted a research goal and produced a plan, candidate source records, a synthesis and an action trace.

The recorded run used a deterministic fallback rather than verified live language-model inference. Live hosted inference remained unverified because the Hugging Face authentication token was invalid. The candidate source records included synthetic placeholder data and must not be treated as verified academic sources.

Design compared with implementation

  • Planning: The local program demonstrated a workflow for turning a goal into planned actions.
  • Retrieval: Academic search services were proposed, but the placeholder records do not demonstrate successful live literature retrieval.
  • Evidence analysis: The critic agent remains a design objective unless supported by further implementation evidence.
  • Reporting: The local run produced a structured synthesis and action trace, but the outputs require validation.
  • Deployment: A fully integrated, production-ready multi-agent service has not been established by the available results.
The practical work demonstrates a limited local workflow, not the complete original proposal. This distinction is important when evaluating project progress.

Development challenges

One challenge was distinguishing a program that runs successfully from a system that produces trustworthy academic evidence. Passing software tests can show that specific functions behave as expected, but it does not establish that sources are real, conclusions are accurate or the system is safe for academic use.

Another challenge was hosted-model authentication. Resolving this would require valid credentials, secure configuration and further testing. Credentials should never be placed directly in public code.

4. Reflective Case Studies

These three case studies use the reflective structure associated with Rolfe, Freshwater and Jasper (2001): What? So what? Now what? They discuss AI safety constraints, explainable decisions and responsible deployment. Personalise them with specific examples from your module activities and experience before submission.

Case Study 1: AI Safety Constraints

What?

The Academic Evidence Investigator is intended to help users investigate academic questions. Such a system may produce incorrect claims, misinterpret source material or present uncertain information too confidently. The local prototype's use of placeholder records illustrates why generated outputs must not automatically be treated as reliable evidence.

So what?

Safety is not only about preventing obviously harmful behaviour. In academic work, an inaccurate citation or unsupported claim can damage the quality and integrity of a student's submission. An agent that continues acting without checking evidence quality may carry errors into later stages of a workflow.

This helped me recognise that technical autonomy and trustworthy autonomy are different. A system may complete several steps independently without being sufficiently reliable to make every decision alone. Its authority should depend on the consequences of an error and the quality of the available information.

Now what?

I would validate source records, record the origin of claims, identify missing evidence and flag uncertainty. The system should request human review when it cannot verify an important claim. Further tests should include irrelevant sources, contradictory evidence and malformed records.

I would also test whether the system communicates its limitations instead of producing a confident but unsupported answer. These measures would make the workflow more cautious and easier to evaluate.

Case Study 2: Explainable Decisions (XAI)

What?

An intelligent research assistant may decide how to divide a research goal, which records to consider, what evidence to summarise and when to recommend another search. Users need enough information to understand these decisions and assess the resulting output.

So what?

A polished answer is not necessarily an explainable one. If a system does not show why it selected a source or how a claim relates to the evidence, users may struggle to identify errors. Academic claims should be traceable to credible sources.

The local program's action trace offers a starting point for making workflow steps visible. However, an action trace alone does not prove that the underlying reasoning is correct. Explanations need to be connected to evidence that can be independently checked.

Now what?

I would provide source details, explain the purpose of major steps and link claims to supporting passages where possible. The system should distinguish retrieved information, generated interpretation and unresolved questions.

I would evaluate explanations by checking whether users can identify the sources behind a conclusion, recognise uncertainty and challenge an incorrect result. This would make explainability a testable requirement rather than simply a visual feature.

Case Study 3: Responsible Deployment

What?

The proposed research assistant could save time during early-stage literature investigation. However, its outputs could be misleading if records are synthetic, outdated, irrelevant or incorrectly summarised. Users may also rely too heavily on automation when its limitations are unclear.

So what?

Responsible deployment requires considering how people actually use a system, not just whether the software runs. A prototype that works in a controlled demonstration may behave differently with real research questions and incomplete evidence.

It is also important to distinguish the original proposal from the implementation achieved. It would be misleading to present proposed features as implemented features or to claim research accuracy without suitable evaluation.

Now what?

Before wider deployment, I would test the system with representative research tasks, use genuine and verifiable source records, document known limitations and establish a process for reporting errors. Users should remain responsible for checking sources and deciding whether conclusions are suitable for academic use.

I would also review privacy and data-handling practices, particularly if users submit unpublished research or personal information. Deployment should follow relevant institutional guidance and take account of risks identified during testing.

Overall reflection

These case studies demonstrate that building an intelligent agent involves more than implementing a workflow. Reliability, transparency and responsible use are also important. My next step is to strengthen the practical evidence and connect each reflection to specific module activities and development experiences.

5. Future Development Plan

  1. Improve evidence quality: replace synthetic records with real, verifiable academic records and validate their metadata.
  2. Resolve model integration: configure authentication securely and verify whether live model inference works as intended.
  3. Strengthen evaluation: test realistic questions, irrelevant sources, contradictory evidence and failure cases.
  4. Improve explainability: make sources, important actions, uncertainty and limitations visible to users.
  5. Complete the portfolio: check that all assignment requirements and supporting evidence are included in the submitted work, even if not displayed as separate sections on this website.

These are proposed improvements, not claims that the work is complete.

6. Conclusion

Developing the Academic Evidence Investigator has provided an opportunity to examine the relationship between intelligent-agent design and practical implementation. The proposed architecture offers a structured way to think about planning, retrieval, evidence analysis and reporting.

The local prototype demonstrated a limited workflow and passed six recorded unit tests. However, deterministic fallback behaviour, unverified live inference and synthetic candidate records limit what can be concluded about its real-world research capabilities.

My main learning point is that an intelligent agent should not be evaluated only by whether it completes a task. Its evidence, uncertainty, safety boundaries and impact on human decisions also matter. Future development should combine technical testing with critical evaluation and responsible oversight.
