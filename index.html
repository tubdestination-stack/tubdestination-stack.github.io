2. Project Overview: Academic Evidence Investigator

The Academic Evidence Investigator was proposed as an intelligent research assistant for postgraduate students and early-career researchers. Its purpose is to help users organise research questions, identify relevant literature, examine evidence and prepare structured summaries.

Academic research requires more than generating fluent text. Researchers need to assess the quality of sources, distinguish evidence from assumptions, recognise uncertainty and verify claims before using them in academic work.

Proposed agent roles

1. Planner / Supervisor Agent

Breaks a research goal into manageable tasks, coordinates the workflow and decides which activity should happen next.

2. Retrieval Agent

Was designed to locate potentially relevant academic sources through services such as Semantic Scholar and Crossref. Retrieved records would need to be checked for accuracy and relevance.

3. Evidence Analysis / Critic Agent

Was designed to examine evidence, identify gaps and inconsistencies, and recommend further investigation when the available information is insufficient.

4. Reporting Agent

Was designed to organise the findings into a structured report, with clear distinctions between evidence, interpretation and unresolved questions.

Intended users and value

The intended users are students and researchers who need support with early-stage literature investigation. The potential value is a more organised research process. However, the system should support academic judgement rather than replace reading, source verification or independent critical thinking.

3. Design and Implementation

Original design proposal

The group proposal considered a multi-agent architecture using LangGraph to coordinate tasks. It proposed Qwen2.5-7B through Hugging Face for language-model capabilities, Semantic Scholar and Crossref for literature discovery, JSON for structured information, SQLite for storage and Flask for a possible web interface.

These technologies describe the proposed design. They should not be interpreted as proof that every proposed component was integrated into the final working implementation.

Practical implementation

The local starter project, named llm_planning_agent_starter, was run in a Python virtual environment. The program accepted a research goal and generated a plan, candidate source records, a synthesis and an action trace.

The implementation used a deterministic fallback path. Live hosted language-model inference was not verified because the Hugging Face authentication token was invalid. The candidate source records included synthetic placeholder data, so they cannot be treated as verified academic sources.

Comparison of design and implementation

  • Planning: The local program demonstrated a workflow for turning a goal into planned actions.
  • Research retrieval: The original design proposed academic search services, but the placeholder records do not demonstrate successful live literature retrieval.
  • Evidence critique: The proposed critic role remains a design objective unless supported by further implementation and testing evidence.
  • Reporting: The local run produced a structured synthesis and action trace, but these outputs require validation before being relied upon.
  • Deployment: A fully integrated, production-ready multi-agent service has not been established by the available test results.
Critical comparison: the practical work demonstrates a limited local workflow, not the complete original proposal. This difference is important when evaluating project progress.

Development challenges

One challenge was distinguishing a program that runs successfully from a system that produces trustworthy research evidence. Passing software tests can demonstrate that specific functions behave as expected, but it cannot independently establish that sources are real, conclusions are accurate or the system is safe for academic use.

A further challenge was hosted-model authentication. Resolving this would require valid credentials, secure configuration and additional tests. Credentials must never be placed directly in publicly accessible source code.

Case Study 1: AI Safety Constraints

What?

The Academic Evidence Investigator is intended to help users investigate academic questions. Such a system may produce incorrect claims, misinterpret source material or present uncertain information too confidently. The local prototype's use of placeholder records illustrates why generated outputs must not automatically be treated as reliable evidence.

So what?

Safety is not only a question of preventing obviously harmful behaviour. In an academic setting, an inaccurate citation or unsupported claim can damage the quality and integrity of a student's work. An agent that continues acting without checking the quality of its evidence may amplify errors across later stages of a workflow.

This changed how I understand autonomy. A system can be technically capable of completing multiple steps without being sufficiently reliable to make every decision alone. Its authority should be limited by the consequences of an error and the quality of the information available.

Now what?

I would introduce clear boundaries for each agent, validate source records, record the origin of claims and flag missing or uncertain evidence. The system should stop and request human review when it cannot verify an important claim. Future testing should include missing information, irrelevant sources, contradictory evidence and deliberately malformed records.

I would also test whether the system correctly communicates its limitations instead of producing a confident but unsupported answer. These measures would make the workflow more cautious and easier to evaluate.

Case Study 2: Explainable Decisions (XAI)

What?

An intelligent research assistant may make several decisions: how to divide a research goal, which records to consider, what evidence to summarise and when to recommend another search. Users need enough information to understand these decisions and assess the resulting output.

So what?

A polished answer is not necessarily an explainable one. If a system does not show why it selected a source or how a claim relates to the available evidence, a user may find it difficult to detect errors. This is particularly important for academic work, where claims should be traceable to credible sources.

The local program's action trace provides a starting point for making workflow steps visible. However, an action trace alone does not prove that the underlying reasoning is correct. Explanations must be connected to evidence that can be independently checked.

Now what?

I would provide source details, explain the purpose of each major step and link individual claims to supporting passages where possible. The interface should distinguish retrieved facts, generated interpretations and unresolved questions.

I would evaluate explanations by asking whether a user can identify the sources behind a conclusion, recognise uncertainty and challenge an incorrect result. This would make explainability a testable requirement rather than a purely visual feature.

Case Study 3: Responsible Deployment

What?

The proposed research assistant could save time during early-stage literature investigation. However, its outputs could be misleading if records are synthetic, outdated, irrelevant or incorrectly summarised. Users may also over-rely on automation when its limitations are unclear.

So what?

Responsible deployment requires considering how people actually use a system, not just whether the software runs. A prototype that works in a controlled demonstration may behave differently with real research questions and incomplete evidence.

The distinction between the original multi-agent proposal and the limited local implementation also matters. It would be misleading to present proposed features as implemented features or to claim research accuracy without suitable evaluation.

Now what?

Before wider deployment, I would test the system with representative research tasks, use genuine and verifiable source records, document known limitations and establish a process for reporting errors. Human users should remain responsible for checking sources and deciding which conclusions are suitable for academic use.

I would also review privacy and data-handling practices, especially if users submit unpublished research or personal information. Deployment should follow appropriate institutional guidance and be informed by the risks discovered during testing.

Overall reflection

These case studies show that building an intelligent agent involves more than implementing a workflow. The system must also be evaluated for reliability, transparency and responsible use. My next step is to strengthen the practical evidence and connect each reflection to a specific module activity or observed development challenge.

8. Conclusion

Developing the Academic Evidence Investigator has provided an opportunity to examine the relationship between intelligent-agent design and practical implementation. The proposed architecture offers a structured way to think about planning, retrieval, evidence analysis and reporting.

The local prototype demonstrated a limited workflow and passed six recorded unit tests. At the same time, the deterministic fallback, unverified live inference and synthetic candidate records limit what can be concluded about its real-world research capabilities.

My main learning point is that an intelligent agent should not be evaluated only by whether it completes a task. Its evidence, uncertainty, safety boundaries and impact on human decisions also matter. Future development should therefore combine technical testing with critical evaluation and responsible oversight.
